Texas vs Oklahoma: how distressed property sales work

Texas forecloses most mortgages without going to court and sells on the first Tuesday of the month; Oklahoma usually forecloses through the courts and sells through the county sheriff after an appraisal. Tax sales differ too: Texas sells after a court judgment with a redemption period afterward, while Oklahoma holds an annual county treasurer resale in June.

TexasOklahoma
Usual mortgage foreclosureNonjudicial (power of sale in the deed of trust), Property Code 51.002Judicial (court case, then sheriff's sale); a power of sale exists but a homestead owner can elect judicial foreclosure, 46 O.S. 43
When sales happenFirst Tuesday of the month, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m.Sheriff's sales are set after judgment; the tax resale is the second Monday of June
Foreclosure noticeAt least 21 days before the sale, posted at the courthouse and filed with the county clerkPublished notice; sale at least 30 days after first publication, 12 O.S. 764
Minimum bid at sheriff saleNo statewide appraisal minimum for mortgage foreclosuresTwo-thirds of appraised value unless appraisal was waived
Tax sale typeTax lien foreclosure by lawsuit, then sheriff or constable sale (Tax Code ch. 34)County treasurer resale of land delinquent 3 or more years (68 O.S. 3105, 3125)
Redemption after a tax sale2 years for homesteads, ag land and mineral interests; 180 days for other property (Tax Code 34.21)See the Oklahoma tax sale guide for the treasurer's process
Small estate shortcutSmall estate affidavit up to $75,000, homestead only for real property (Estates Code ch. 205)Affidavit up to $50,000 (58 O.S. 393); summary administration up to $200,000 (58 O.S. 245)

Each row is explained, with the statute links, in the state guides: Texas guides and Oklahoma guides.
