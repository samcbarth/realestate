How does a property end up at a Texas tax sale?

Unpaid property taxes create a lien. To turn that lien into a sale, the taxing unit (or the attorneys it hires) usually files a lawsuit and gets a judgment of foreclosure. The court then issues an order of sale. The Tax Code also allows a sale after property is seized under a tax warrant. Either way, the sale is run by "the officer charged with selling the property", which in practice is the sheriff or a constable.

When and where are the auctions?

Under Tax Code Section 34.01, tax sales follow the rules for sales under execution: at the county courthouse, or another public place near it designated by the commissioners court, between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m. on the first Tuesday of the month. If the first Tuesday is January 1 or July 4, the sale moves to the first Wednesday.

A commissioners court can also authorize online auctions. An online auction may open at any time but must close at 4 p.m. on the sale day. Whether a particular county sells in person or online is a local choice, so check with the county's tax office or the officer conducting the sale.

What is the minimum bid?

For property sold after a tax lien foreclosure, the officer needs a bid at least equal to the lesser of the total amount due under the judgment (taxes, penalties, interest, court costs and sale costs) or the value the court adjudged. If no bid reaches that amount, the property is "struck off" to the taxing unit that requested the sale.

What happens to struck-off property?

Under Section 34.05, a taxing unit that bought property at a tax sale can resell it later, either at a public sale run like an execution sale (or an online auction where authorized), or at a private sale. A private sale generally cannot be for less than the lesser of the market value in the judgment or the total amount of the judgments. These resales are a separate source of deals that some investors watch.

How does redemption work?

Buying at a Texas tax sale does not end the former owner's rights right away. Section 34.21 gives the owner a right to buy the property back:

Property typeRedemption periodPremium paid to the buyer
Residence homestead, land designated for agricultural use, mineral interest2 years from the date the buyer's deed is filed25% in year one, 50% in year two
All other property180 days from the date the buyer's deed is filedUp to 25%

On top of the premium, the owner repays the bid amount, the deed recording fee, and taxes, penalties, interest and costs the buyer paid. For an investor, that means the money can come back with a premium, or the property can be kept after the period ends. Plan for both outcomes.

What to check before you bid

  • Read the judgment and the sale notice for the exact property and the liens being foreclosed.
  • Find out whether the property was a homestead or ag land, since that sets a 2-year redemption period.
  • Ask a title company before the sale whether it will insure title after a tax sale and on what conditions.
  • Drive by the property. Many tax-sale properties are occupied or need work, and you cannot inspect inside before bidding.
  • Budget for holding costs during the redemption period and run the numbers with the fix and flip calculator.
