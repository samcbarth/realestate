Why probate property is different

When an owner dies, nobody can sell their real estate until someone has legal authority to sign the deed. Probate property often comes with motivated sellers: heirs who live out of state, need to divide proceeds, or cannot afford upkeep. The catch for buyers is that the paperwork has to be right, or a title company will not insure the sale.

The main paths to a sellable title in Texas

Executor or administrator appointed by the court

When a court opens an administration, it appoints a personal representative. Depending on the type of administration and what the will allows, that person may be able to sell real estate with or without further court approval. The appointment papers (letters testamentary or letters of administration) show who can sign.

Will probated as a muniment of title

A court can admit a will to probate as a muniment of title, without a full administration, when it is satisfied the estate owes no unpaid debts other than those secured by liens on real estate, or finds no other need for administration (Estates Code 257.001). The beneficiaries named in the will then sell as owners.

Small estate affidavit

For estates worth $75,000 or less, not counting the homestead and exempt property, heirs can use a small estate affidavit approved by the court (Chapter 205). It can pass title to a homestead, but not to other real estate, so it does not help with rentals or vacant lots.

Affidavit of heirship

When someone dies without a will, an affidavit of heirship lays out the family history to show who inherited. Recorded in the deed records for 5 years or more, it becomes prima facie evidence of those facts in title disputes (Chapter 203). Title companies set their own rules for when they will rely on one, especially if the affidavit is newer.

Watch the 4-year clock

Texas generally requires a will to be offered for probate before the fourth anniversary of the death (Estates Code Chapter 256). After that, the will usually can only be used in limited ways, which can push heirs into the heirship route. If you are talking with heirs about a property where the owner died years ago, find out early which path applies.

Practical checklist for buyers

  • Get the names of every heir or beneficiary and confirm who has authority to sign.
  • Open title with a title company early and ask what it needs to insure.
  • Check for unpaid property taxes, which keep accruing after death. The Texas tax sale guide explains what happens if they go unpaid.
  • Run the numbers with the fix and flip calculator; inherited homes often need deferred maintenance.

This is general information about Texas law, not legal advice. Probate questions about a specific estate belong with a Texas probate attorney.
